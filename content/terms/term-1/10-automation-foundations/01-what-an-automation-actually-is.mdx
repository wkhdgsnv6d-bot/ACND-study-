Every automation, on every platform, is the same three things: something starts
it, a sequence of steps does work, and data travels between them.

Learn it in those terms and a tool you have never opened is only unfamiliar in
its buttons.

## The model

**A trigger** starts a run.

**Actions** do the work, in order. Fetch a record, reshape it, create something,
send a message.

**A payload** travels between them, changing shape at each step. What comes out
of step three is what step four receives — and most debugging is discovering
that what came out was not what you assumed.

That is the whole model. Platforms differ in what they call the pieces — nodes,
steps, blocks — and in how much they hide from you, but a workflow described as
trigger-actions-data can be rebuilt anywhere.

This matters commercially. If a client's automation lives only as a diagram of
one vendor's nodes, they are locked to that vendor. If it is documented as a
model, moving is a rebuild rather than a rescue.

## The four triggers

**Event triggers** fire when something happens elsewhere — a webhook arriving.
Fast, cheap while idle, and dependent on the source system offering one.

**Schedule triggers** fire at a fixed interval. Right for anything time-based
rather than event-based: reports, reconciliation, digests, and anything where
the source cannot notify you.

**Manual triggers** are run by a person. Undervalued. Plenty of client work is
"do this thing correctly, when I decide", and putting a human at the start
removes a whole category of unattended-failure risk.

**Chained triggers** fire when another workflow finishes. Useful for splitting a
long job into pieces that can be retried independently — but each link couples
two workflows, so a failure upstream silently stops everything downstream.

Choosing is mostly two questions: *how quickly does this need to happen*, and
*can the source system tell me at all*.

## The three questions before you build

Not every process should be automated, and knowing which is part of the job.

### 1. Can it be fully described?

If the person doing it cannot write down the rules, the rules are not rules —
they are judgement, and judgement does not survive being automated. It comes
out as either an automation that does the wrong thing confidently, or one so
hedged with exceptions that a human reviews every run anyway.

The test: ask them to write the process down. What comes back reveals the
exceptions they handle by instinct and never thought to mention. Those
exceptions are the actual specification.

### 2. Does the frequency justify it?

Do the arithmetic honestly, including maintenance:

> 15 minutes, twice a month = 6 hours a year.
> Building it properly: a day. Keeping it working: a few hours a year.

That does not pay back in year one, and it adds one more thing that can break.
Compare with:

> 3 minutes, forty times a day = 130 hours a year.

That is worth a week of work.

The bias worth resisting: automating is more interesting than the task, and the
build cost is felt once while the saving is imagined over and over.

### 3. What happens when it goes wrong unattended?

This is the question that separates a hobby automation from professional work.

Your workflow runs at 3am on data you have never seen, with credentials that
can change a real business's records. So: what is the worst thing it could do?

If the answer involves money leaving, messages reaching customers, or records
being deleted, then either a human approves that step, or you build the
safeguards deliberately and tell the client they exist. An automation that can
email every contact in a CRM is one malformed payload away from doing exactly
that.

## Talking to clients about it

Clients hear "automated" and picture something that cannot fail. It is worth
correcting gently, early, in four sentences:

- **What starts it** — "when someone submits the booking form".
- **How quickly** — "within about ten seconds".
- **What it does** — "creates the contact and the deal, and messages the team".
- **What happens when it fails** — "it retries three times, then puts it in a
  list and emails you; nothing is lost".

That fourth sentence is the one people skip, and it is the one that sets whether
the client trusts you when something does fail. Which it will.

## What to take away

Trigger, actions, data. Four trigger types, chosen on latency and on whether
the source can tell you. And three questions before building anything: can it be
described, does the frequency justify it, and what happens when it goes wrong
with nobody watching. Being willing to answer the third honestly — sometimes
with "do not automate this" — is what makes you worth hiring.
