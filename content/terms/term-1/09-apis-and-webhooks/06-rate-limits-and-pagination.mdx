    prompt: "How do you decide whether a proposed integration fits inside an API's rate limit?"
    answer: "Multiply out the worst realistic case, not the average. Requests per run, times runs per period, plus any per-record calls the design implies — a sync of 500 contacts that fetches details individually is 500 requests, not one. Compare that against the documented limit and check the window it applies to, because 100 per minute and 100 per day are wildly different constraints.   - key: pagination-styles
    prompt: "What are the two main pagination styles, how do you recognise each, and which is safer?"
    answer: "Offset pagination asks by position — limit and offset, or a page number — and is recognisable by those parameters. Cursor pagination asks for what comes after an opaque marker, usually returned as a next_cursor or a link in the response. Cursor is safer: offsets are positions in a list that can change between requests, so a record inserted mid-loop shifts everything down and you skip one. Cursors point at a place in the data rather than a count, so insertions do not disturb them."
  - key: sync-design
    prompt: "What makes a sync survive a client's data growing tenfold?"
    answer: "Fetching only what changed rather than everything — an incremental sync keyed on an updated-since timestamp or a cursor stored between runs. A full download that works at 500 records is 5,000 requests at 50,000, and it will breach the rate limit, take longer than the interval between runs, and eventually overlap with itself. Incremental syncs stay roughly constant in cost regardless of total data size, because they are proportional to the change, not the corpus."

Two constraints decide whether an integration is possible at all, and both are
visible in the documentation before you write anything. People discover them
after building instead, which is why this lesson exists.

## Rate limits

A rate limit caps how many requests you may make in a period. `100 requests per
minute`, `1,000 per hour`, `10,000 per day`.

The critical detail is the **window**, because 100 per minute and 100 per day
differ by a factor of 1,440. Read which one it is before you design anything.

### Do the arithmetic before you quote

Take the design you have in mind, and multiply out the worst realistic case:

> Sync 2,000 contacts every 15 minutes. List them 50 at a time — 40 requests.
> Then fetch each contact's full detail — 2,000 requests. Total: **2,040 per
> run.**
>
> Limit is 100 per minute. That is over 20 minutes of pure request budget for a
> job scheduled every 15.

The design does not fit, and no amount of retry logic fixes it. Each run
overlaps the next and the backlog grows forever.

Notice which number dominates: the per-record calls, not the list. That is
almost always where the volume is, and almost always what gets forgotten in the
estimate. If you take one habit from this section, take this: **count the calls
your design makes per record, not per run.**

### When you hit the limit

You get `429 Too Many Requests`, usually with headers:

```
HTTP/1.1 429 Too Many Requests
Retry-After: 30
X-RateLimit-Limit: 100
X-RateLimit-Remaining: 0
X-RateLimit-Reset: 1786982400
```

`Retry-After` is the server telling you exactly how long to wait. Obey it. It
beats any backoff schedule you invented, because it is based on information you
do not have.

`X-RateLimit-Remaining` is more useful still, because it lets you avoid the 429
entirely — slow down as it approaches zero rather than sprinting into the wall.
That is **throttling**, and it is the difference between an integration that
works and one that works between rejections.

### Limits are shared

This is the part that causes a support call you did not expect.

Rate limits are usually **per account**, not per integration. If a client has
their website chat widget, their accounting sync and your automation all hitting
the same API, you share one budget. An automation that consumes the whole quota
breaks their other systems — and nothing about that outage points at you.

Ask what else connects to the API before deploying anything high-volume, and
leave headroom.

## Pagination

APIs do not return 50,000 records in one response. They return a page, and a way
to ask for the next.

### The failure mode

The first page is valid JSON containing real records. On your test account,
which has twelve contacts, it is also *all* the records. Everything works.

Then it runs against a client with 4,000 contacts and processes the first 50.

Nothing errors. The workflow shows green. **Always check for a next page, even
when testing against data small enough not to have one.**

### Offset pagination

The intuitive model: skip some, take some.

```
GET /contacts?limit=50&offset=0
GET /contacts?limit=50&offset=50
GET /contacts?limit=50&offset=100
```

Loop until you get a page with fewer records than the limit, or an empty one.

The problem is that an offset is a **position in a list that can change**.
Insert a record at the top while you are on page 3, and everything shifts down
one — so the record that was last on page 3 is now first on page 4, and the one
that was first on page 4 is never returned to anyone.

You skipped a record. No error. Every page had 50 valid entries.

This is worst when sorting newest-first on actively changing data, which is
exactly what a sync does.

### Cursor pagination

The safer model: *give me what comes after this marker.*

```json
{
  "data": [ ... ],
  "meta": { "has_more": true, "next_cursor": "eyJpZCI6MTIwMn0" }
}
```

You send the cursor back to get the next page, and loop while `has_more` is
true.

A cursor points at a **place in the data**, not a count. Inserting a record does
not move it. This is why every well-designed modern API uses cursors, and why
you should prefer them wherever both are offered.

Some APIs put the pointer in a `Link` header rather than the body. Same idea,
different envelope.

### If you must use offsets

Sort by something immutable and ascending — `id`, or `created_at` — rather than
by recency. New records then land at the *end*, past the pages you have already
walked, and cannot shift the ones underneath you.

## Designing for growth

A sync that downloads everything works fine at 500 records and dies at 50,000.
The fix is to fetch **only what changed**:

```
GET /contacts?updated_since=2026-08-11T04:00:00Z
```

Store the timestamp of the last successful run. Ask for changes since then.
Twenty changes a day costs twenty records to process whether the client has
2,000 contacts or 40,000 — the cost tracks the *change*, not the *corpus*.

Three details that decide whether this survives contact with reality:

**The first run is different.** It has to walk the whole collection once.
Treat it as a one-off backfill: paginated, throttled well below the limit,
ideally out of hours.

**Store the marker durably**, not in memory. A workflow that loses its
timestamp on restart either re-downloads everything or, worse, silently skips
the gap.

**Handle a half-finished run.** If the sync fails at record 3,000 of 5,000, it
must resume rather than either starting over or advancing the marker past
records it never processed. Only advance the marker on success.

## What to take away

Multiply the arithmetic out before quoting, counting per-record calls. Obey
`Retry-After` and throttle before you hit the wall. Always loop pages, even
when testing on data too small to have any. Prefer cursors. And design the sync
for the client's data in two years, because that is when it will be judged and
you will not be watching.
