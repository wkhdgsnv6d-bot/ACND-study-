Every API call you make on behalf of a client carries a credential to that
client's business. Their customer list, their invoices, their bookings. It is
worth understanding what you are holding.

## Two different questions

**Authentication** asks *who are you*. **Authorisation** asks *what are you
allowed to do*.

They are separate, and separating them is not pedantry — it is a debugging
shortcut you will use constantly:

- `401 Unauthorized` — your credential was not accepted. Wrong key, expired
  token, malformed header. **Identity problem.**
- `403 Forbidden` — your credential was accepted and the answer is still no.
  Missing scope, insufficient permissions. **Permission problem.**

The names are unhelpfully swapped from what you would expect, which is a known
wart in HTTP. Learn the pair once: **401 means we do not know you, 403 means we
know you and no.**

When one endpoint fails with 403 while others succeed, you are not looking at a
credential problem at all. Rotating the key will not help.

## The three schemes

### API keys

A long string, sent with each request:

```
Authorization: Bearer sk_live_51H...
X-API-Key: 8f4c2e...
```

Simple, and the most common thing you will meet on small-business software.
Two things to know:

They usually do not expire, which is convenient and means an exposed key stays
dangerous indefinitely.

They usually carry **broad access**. Many vendors issue one key per account
with full permissions. Some offer scoped or restricted keys — always ask, and
always take the narrower one.

### Bearer tokens

"Bearer" means what it sounds like: whoever holds it, gets in. There is no
second factor and no proof of identity beyond possession of the string.

This is why transport matters. A bearer token over plain HTTP is readable by
anything between you and the server. Always HTTPS, always in a header, never in
a query string — a query string ends up in server logs, proxy logs, browser
history and the `Referer` header sent to third parties.

### OAuth

OAuth solves a problem the first two do not: **letting a user grant your
application access to their account without giving you their password.**

The flow you will use is the authorisation code grant. Explained the way you
would explain it to a client:

1. You send them to the vendor's own login page — HubSpot's, Google's, Xero's.
2. They log in **there**. You never see the password.
3. The vendor shows them exactly what your application is asking to do:
   "Ascend Automation wants to read your contacts."
4. If they agree, the vendor sends them back to your application with a
   one-time code.
5. Your **server** swaps that code for an access token, using a client secret
   only your server knows.
6. You act on their behalf, within what they agreed, until they revoke it.

Two properties make this worth the extra work. The client never hands over a
password, and they can revoke your access from their own settings without
involving you. Both are things you can say in a sales conversation, and both
are true.

The cost: OAuth is more work to set up. You need a registered application, a
redirect URL, and sometimes a review by the vendor before you can use it with
real customers. **Find this out during scoping, not during delivery** — vendor
review can take weeks.

### Access tokens and refresh tokens

OAuth gives you two things. The **access token** is short-lived — often an
hour — and is what you send with requests. The **refresh token** is long-lived
and is used to get a new access token when the old one expires.

The implication for automation: your integration must handle expiry. A workflow
that fetches a token once and holds it will work perfectly in testing and fail
in the middle of the night, some time after you have moved on.

## Scopes, and why narrow is worth the friction

A scope is a permission attached to a token: `contacts.read`, `deals.write`,
`admin`.

The temptation is to request everything. Full access always works, and under
deadline pressure it removes a debugging step. The reason not to is simple: if
that credential leaks, the scope you chose is the difference between "somebody
could read their deal names" and "somebody could delete their customer
database".

Start with the narrowest scope you think will work. When something fails with
403, widen it. That failure costs a minute and tells you precisely what the
integration needs — which is also what you will write in the handover
document.

## Holding a client's credentials

You will be given credentials to systems containing a business's most sensitive
data. Treat them accordingly.

**Never commit one.** Environment variables, or the secret store of whatever
platform runs the automation. Bots scrape public commits for credential
patterns within minutes of a push.

**Never paste one into a chat**, including a chat with an AI assistant. Chats
are logged, searchable, and often retained.

**Never let one into a screenshot.** This is the one people miss — a screenshot
of a working configuration, sent to show progress, with the key in frame.

**Ask for scoped keys** where the vendor supports them.

**Document where it lives**, so the client can rotate it after you hand over.
An integration whose credentials only you can change is a dependency you have
created for them, not a service.

## When it leaks

It will happen to someone you work with, and possibly to you. The response
matters more than the mistake.

1. **Rotate immediately.** Before investigating. Rotation stops future use and
   costs a redeploy.
2. **Check the audit log** for the whole exposure window. Rotation says nothing
   about what already happened, and "I rotated it" is not an answer to "was it
   used".
3. **Treat it as compromised regardless of cleanup.** Rewriting git history
   does not un-scrape a key that was public for nine days.
4. **Tell the client the same day.** What was exposed, for how long, what you
   found, what you have done.

That last step is the one people delay, and delaying it is what turns a
recoverable mistake into a lost client. A client can forgive an error you
brought to them. They will not forgive finding out from their accountant.

## What to take away

401 is identity, 403 is permission. API keys are long-lived and usually
over-privileged. OAuth costs more to build and gives the client control they
can see. Request the narrowest scope that works. And handle the leak you hope
never happens by deciding, now, that you will report it the same day.
