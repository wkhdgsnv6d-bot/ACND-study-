Three things separate an automation you built from one a client can rely on:
proving it works, knowing it still works, and handing it over.

## Test the cases that break it

The automation works for the case you built it for. That is not information —
you would not have shipped otherwise.

What you need to know is what happens on the other inputs. Write the cases down
*before* running them, with the expected outcome, so you are testing rather than
observing.

**Real data first.** Export a hundred real records from the client's system and
run them through. This finds more bugs than any invented case, because reality
contains inputs you would not think to invent: the customer whose surname is
one letter, the address with a line break in it, the phone number someone typed
with a leading apostrophe to stop a spreadsheet mangling it.

Invented test data comes from the same mental model that built the workflow. It
tests what you already thought of.

**Then the edges.** Empty optional field. Missing field. Zero. Maximum length.
First and last day of a month. A timezone boundary. A name with no space. An
apostrophe. Non-Latin characters.

**Then the failures.** Same trigger twice. Each dependency unavailable. Bad
credentials. A batch ten times normal size. A malformed payload.

**Then the recovery.** Fix a dead-lettered record and replay it. Confirm it
succeeds and leaves exactly one result.

Twelve to twenty cases covers most automations. It takes an afternoon and it is
the afternoon that decides whether you hear from this client at 8pm.

## Working without a sandbox

Most small-business software has no test environment. Refusing the work is not
an answer; working carefully is.

- **Create your own test records** — a contact and a booking that are yours, so
  a mistake damages nothing real.
- **Keep it read-only, or write to a scratch destination**, until the
  transformation is proven correct.
- **Agree a window with the client**, so an odd record appearing is expected
  rather than alarming.
- **Know how to undo** each write before you make it.

And price it accordingly. Careful is slower, and a client who understands why is
usually fine with it.

## Monitor for silence, not just errors

Here is the failure that costs clients, and it produces no errors at all.

The form field gets renamed. The webhook gets deleted during an unrelated
change. The schedule gets disabled by someone tidying up. The workflow simply
stops being triggered.

Nothing errors. Nothing alerts. Everything shows green, because nothing ran.
Eleven days later the client asks why no leads have come through.

**Error monitoring cannot see this.** You need something watching for the
*absence* of success:

- The workflow pings a heartbeat URL on every successful run.
- The heartbeat service alerts when the ping does not arrive within the expected
  interval.

Free services do this and it takes ten minutes. For a workflow that should run
at least daily, a missed heartbeat is a real signal — and it is the only signal
you will get.

For event-driven workflows, set the expected interval from the actual pattern.
If a client normally gets five bookings a day, silence for 48 hours is worth an
alert. That threshold comes from their business, so ask.

Monitor three things in total: **errors** (something broke), **silence**
(nothing is running), and **volume** (a tenth of normal is a symptom even
without an error).

## Handover

The difference between a service and a dependency.

Five things:

**1. The design, in plain language.** The same document the client approved. What
starts it, what it does, what happens when it fails.

**2. The runbook.** The routine problems and what to do. Mostly: a record failed,
fix the data, tick retry.

**3. Access to the evidence.** The dead-letter list, the monitoring dashboard,
the run history. A client who can see it working does not have to take your
word for it — and stops asking.

**4. Credential ownership.** This is the one people get wrong. Everything should
run on accounts **in the client's name**, with you granted access — not the
reverse. If it runs on your account, they cannot audit it, cannot remove you,
and lose the integration if you disappear. Include how to revoke your access,
and offer it explicitly. It costs nothing and tells them exactly what kind of
supplier you are.

**5. What they can change themselves.** Be specific: the alert email address,
the message wording, who is on the alert list. Show them before you finish.
Equally, be honest about what needs someone technical.

## Then decide what support is

After handover, the client will have questions. Decide in advance what you are
selling, and write it down:

- **Nothing.** Fine, if said clearly at the start.
- **Bug fixes for a period.** If it does not do what was agreed, you fix it, for
  30 or 90 days.
- **A retainer.** Monitoring, small changes, a response time. This is the
  Ascend Essential and Growth model, and it is what turns project revenue into
  recurring revenue.

The thing to avoid is the undefined middle: informal favours that started as one
quick fix. They are much easier to grant than to renegotiate, and a year later
you are maintaining six clients' automations for nothing while wondering where
your time goes.

## What to take away

Test with real data before invented data, and write the expected outcome first.
Monitor for silence, because the worst failure produces no errors. Hand over the
design, the runbook, the evidence and the credentials — in the client's name,
with your access revocable. And decide what support is before the first question
arrives.
