Something happened in one system. Another system needs to know. There are
exactly two ways to arrange that, and the choice shapes the latency, the cost
and the failure modes of everything downstream.

## Asking, versus being told

**Polling** is checking the letterbox every ten minutes. You ask the API "has
anything changed?", it usually says no, and you ask again shortly.

**Webhooks** are being telephoned when the post arrives. You give the vendor a
URL, and they send a request to it when the event happens.

That is the whole distinction. Everything below follows from it.

## What polling costs you

Polling burns requests whether or not anything happened. Every five minutes is
288 requests a day; every minute is 1,440. Almost all of them return "nothing
new".

It also sets a floor on latency. Poll every fifteen minutes and your average
delay is seven and a half minutes, worst case fifteen. You cannot do better
without polling more often, and polling more often costs more requests — the
trade-off is direct and unavoidable.

Polling also requires you to **detect** the change yourself. The API gives you
the current state; working out what is new means either storing what you saw
last time, or relying on an `updated_since` filter if the vendor offers one.
That is state you now have to keep somewhere durable, and get right.

What polling gives you in return is genuine and often undersold:

- **No public endpoint needed.** Your automation reaches out; nothing reaches
  in.
- **You control the timing.** It runs when you say, at a rate you choose.
- **It works with anything.** Every API supports being read. Not every API
  supports webhooks.
- **It is self-healing.** Miss a run, and the next one still sees the current
  state. There is nothing to lose.

## What webhooks cost you

Webhooks are near-instant and cost nothing while idle, which makes them the
obvious choice for anything a person is waiting on.

The cost is that **you no longer control when data arrives**, and that hands
you three failure modes polling does not have.

**Duplicates.** Delivery is at-least-once, not exactly-once. If your endpoint
is slow to respond, the vendor assumes failure and retries — even though you
already processed it. This shows up as rare, unreproducible duplicate records,
maybe one in a few hundred, with no bug in your logic. The fix is not in the
logic: it is to record the event id you have already handled and ignore a
repeat.

**Out of order.** Two events fired half a second apart can arrive in either
order. If you process "deal updated to Closed Won" before "deal updated to
Negotiation", you end up with the wrong state and no error anywhere.

**Missed entirely.** If your endpoint is down beyond the vendor's retry window,
the event is gone. Silently — nothing arrives, so nothing alerts. This is the
one that gets discovered by the client.

And a webhook endpoint is a **publicly reachable URL that accepts data and acts
on it**. That is a security surface, and it is what the next lesson is about.

## Choosing

Use a **webhook** when:

- The vendor offers one for the event you need, and
- The requirement is time-sensitive — a customer or a colleague is waiting.

Use **polling** when:

- The vendor has no webhook for that event, or
- You need periodic reconciliation rather than reaction, or
- You cannot configure the source system at all.

And then, for anything that matters:

## Use both

The strongest design for a client system is almost always a webhook for speed
plus an infrequent poll for safety.

The webhook handles the normal case in seconds. A reconciliation poll — hourly,
or nightly — asks "anything changed since the last thing I processed?" and
catches whatever the webhook missed, whether the cause was a deploy, an outage,
a vendor incident or a bug you have not found yet.

This is not belt and braces. It is the difference between a system whose
failures are self-correcting and one whose failures are permanent and invisible.
The poll is cheap precisely because it is infrequent — you are not using it for
latency, only for completeness.

## Talking to clients about it

Clients do not ask about mechanisms. They ask why something is slow, or why the
new automation costs more to run than the old one.

The honest framing: *the old one checked twice a day, so a booking made at nine
in the morning might not reach you until evening. This one is told when it
happens.* Then be straight about the cost, including that most of it is usually
the platform's base fee rather than the traffic.

And offer the real choice: if a half-day delay genuinely does not matter for
some particular thing, move that one to a schedule and save the cost. What you
are avoiding is accepting a half-day delay everywhere by default, because nobody
made a decision.

## What to take away

Polling asks; webhooks tell. Polling costs requests continuously and has a
latency floor you cannot beat. Webhooks are fast and free while idle, and give
you duplicates, reordering and silent loss to design around. Assume
at-least-once, key your processing on the event id, and put a cheap
reconciliation poll behind anything a client depends on.
